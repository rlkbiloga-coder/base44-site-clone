Content analysis app templates for your own data

General analysis tools are built for general documents, which is why they stall on yours. Your contracts have clauses that matter to your business, your support tickets have categories only your team uses, and the answer you need isn't a summary. These content analysis app templates are working apps you clone and point at your own material. You define the questions, the fields and the checks, and the same question gets asked of every item.

Frequently asked questions

A content analysis app template is a pre-built application that reads unstructured material and returns structured output, which you clone and adapt to your own sources. You give it documents, messages or pages; it gives you fields, categories, scores or summaries you can sort and count. The reason to build your own rather than use a general tool is that the useful questions are specific to your business, and so are the checks you need before you trust the answer.

Anything where the same question has to be asked of a lot of material:

  • A contract reviewer — renewal dates, notice periods, the clauses you care about.
  • A feedback analyzer — tickets and reviews sorted into your own categories.
  • A competitor watcher — specific pages checked for specific changes.
  • An invoice or receipt reader — fields out of PDFs and into your records.
  • A research assistant — a body of documents you can actually interrogate.
  • A compliance checker — flagging what doesn't match a standard you define.

Define the output shape before you process anything. A vague question asked of a thousand documents produces a thousand vague answers, and you'll only discover that afterwards.

Text-based sources are straightforward: PDFs with real text, documents, spreadsheets, CSVs, emails, web pages and anything behind an API. Two honest limits. Scanned documents and photos need OCR first, and that step sets the ceiling on everything after it. And tables inside PDFs extract far less reliably than prose — if your value is tabular, test with your messiest real file rather than your cleanest.

Good enough to change how you work, not good enough to trust blindly — and the advantage of building your own app is that you can design the checking in. Clearly stated facts extract reliably; judgement calls and anything implied are much weaker. Keep a link from every extracted value back to its source passage, flag low-confidence items for a human, and hand-check a random sample each run. If a wrong answer would be expensive, a person sees it.

Yes, and that's when it stops being a one-off exercise. The app can watch a folder, an inbox or a set of pages, process only what's new, and alert you when something crosses a line you've set — a contract approaching notice, a competitor changing a price. scheduled tasks cover how that runs.

Into your own app's database first, so you can query it, correct it and re-ask the question later without reprocessing everything. From there it can go anywhere useful — a dashboard, a spreadsheet for whoever prefers one, your CRM, or an alert in chat. Browse the available integrations.

Choose on the question, not the pile:

  • What decision changes — if none does, don't build it.
  • Source quality — clean text, messy PDFs or scans? This sets the difficulty.
  • Volume — fifty documents once, or five hundred a week?
  • Output shape — fields, categories, or prose?
  • Cost of error — how bad is one wrong extraction?

Clone the closest one, run twenty real documents, and check every one by hand. If you mainly need to display numbers you already hold rather than extract new ones, the BI and analytics templates are the better start — or describe what you need to the business app builder.

Go ahead. Build it yourself.
